PART I — SHARED ARCHITECTURE

1. Competition Structure

Two rounds, one child each, played on the live simulation: a ventilator screen the team touches and a patient monitor the team watches, both driven by the same physiology and run from the facilitator panel.

Comparison is within-round only. The two teams playing the same round must face precisely the same scenario — identical stages at identical minutes, identical prompts, identical scripted answers, identical information released at identical moments. This is why the clock now drives the case, and why the resource-answer tables in §7 and §9 exist.

Sequestration

  • Teams not currently competing are held in a sealed room with no feed and phones surrendered.
  • No debrief for either team until both teams in that round have played.

The two rounds are mirror images: one lung is too stiff to stretch, the other too obstructed to empty. Both test reading the ventilator's measured values rather than its alarms.

3. How the Clock Runs the Case

Governing principle. Every stage and scripted event fires on the clock for both teams. Team decisions modulate severity and resolution — never occurrence.
  • Start the clock on the facilitator panel as the stretcher enters. From then on the panel moves the stages, fires the acute event and shows each confederate line in a Say now box at its minute.
  • Severity: the team's settings and orders run through the physiology on all screens. A child who reaches a stage after a worse or life-threatening decision arrives sicker. A child on lung-protective settings looks better within the same stage.
  • Resolution: the acute event resolves only through the team's actions (sedation in Round 1, disconnection and a lower rate in Round 2).
  • Overtaking: intubation happens at the tube-in minute for every team. A team that has not decided is overtaken by the consultant, scored as a delay.
  • Pause only for a technical failure. Clock off returns the panel to manual stages (teaching, not competition).

Confederate behavioural ceilings

  • Never make a clinical decision or offer a diagnosis. The nurse does not say "her CO₂ is going up" or "should we intubate". She reports only what she is asked, and the fixed prompts.
  • The nurse sets the ventilator only when told, and reads back. The ventilator screen is the team's: the nurse may press Confirm for them, never choose a value.
  • The parent never obstructs care. She escalates emotionally if ignored and does not leave, but never physically interferes or refuses.
  • The consultant on the phone advises only when asked, and acts only at the overtaking minute.
  • No unprompted findings. Every examination finding and result is available when asked for, and volunteered by nobody.

Safety valve

If by 8:00 a team has become non-functional and no meaningful care is happening, the Chief Judge signals the nurse to escalate prompts heavily. No team should score near zero because of a coordination failure in the opening minutes; the value lies in what they do once oriented.

4. Judging Model

Four judges: two clinical, two non-clinical. One non-scoring Chief.

DO NOT SUM THE TWO SHEETS IN A DOMAIN. AVERAGE THEM. Two judges each scoring Domain I out of 40 produce a domain mark out of 40, not 80.

Disagreement signal. If the two judges in a domain differ by more than 6 marks in Domain I or 8 marks in Domain II, hold a thirty-second conference before totals are locked.

The Chief does not score. The role is: hold the flat 15:00 clock, watch that the panel fires on time, trigger the safety valve, adjudicate the tiebreaker, and preside over both rounds so one standard applies throughout.

Tiebreaker

Pre-declared. 60 seconds. Team leader only, no conferring.

Round 1: "Your PEEP is 12, FiO₂ 1.0, OI 26. Tell me the order of your next three steps, and what would make you skip straight to the last one."

Round 2: "The blood pressure falls to 60 systolic, two minutes after intubation. Tell me what you do in the first thirty seconds, and the five causes you work through after that."

Weaning in asthma is usually fast: as the airways open, the trapped volume falls, and a child who was unventilatable yesterday is often extubated within 24–48 hours.If the team disconnected and restarted with a lower rate and VT, move the panel to stage 7 (stable) at 13:00; otherwise leave the event physiology running. Plan and handover. Hard stop 15:00.

Other choices on the panel:
Keep a blocker infusion for 48 hours for safety → worse
Raise the rate to 24 now that the CO₂ is only 50 → worse
Extubate now while he is deeply sedated → life-threatening
What the screens do. The stage moves on the clock. The team's settings and orders run through the physiology on every screen: a good decision makes the child better within the stage, a harmful one makes the child worse, and a stage that ended worse or life-threatening carries a sicker child into the next one. 14. Debrief — Both Rounds

No debrief runs until both teams in that round have played.

Open with the design reveal. State that every stage and the acute event fired on the clock, and that both teams faced the identical arc. Then show the panel's Debrief tab: their own settings, with where each one took the child.

Then, by objective:

  • Opening — how are you feeling? What was the hardest moment?
  • Leadership — walk me through the first sixty seconds. Who led, who had the airway, who had the ventilator?
  • Anchoring — R1: she was alert and drinking with a pH of 7.35. What made you worry? R2: the crew said he was settling. What did the quiet chest tell you?
  • The support decision — R1: what were your NIV failure criteria, and when did you write them down? R2: what made intubation a clinical decision?
  • The first settings — what did you set, and how did you check it was safe? What did the plateau and the driving pressure (R1), or the expiratory hold (R2), tell you?
  • The middle — R1: how did you choose the PEEP, and what told you to stop? R2: the CO₂ was 78. Why leave it?
  • The acute event — R1: the alarm and the fighting: what did you check before you sedated? R2: the collapse: what did you do in the first ten seconds, and why?
  • Communication — the parent arrived at the worst moment. How did you manage that without stopping?
  • Closing — one thing you would change about how you organised the team.

Take-aways the debrief should land

Round 1

  • NIV is a trial with a stop rule. Write the failure criteria down before you start.
  • Intubation in ARDS is a resuscitation: fluids, pressor and a cuffed tube ready.
  • Any mode works. The limits are what matter: VT 6–8 mL/kg, plateau ≤ 28–30, driving pressure ≤ 15.
  • Oxygenation = FiO₂ + mean airway pressure. PEEP first, then inspiratory time, both checked against the flow scalar.
  • Titrate PEEP to best compliance or lowest driving pressure, and watch the blood pressure.
  • Asynchrony: check the tube, secretions and chest first, then sedation, then the briefest paralysis.
  • Outside the ventilator: fluids, infection, haemoglobin, haemodynamics, abdomen, chest wall.
  • Rescue ladder: prone → HFOV → ECMO discussion. Accept SpO₂ 88–92% and a high CO₂ with pH ≥ 7.20.

Round 2

  • Intubation in asthma is a clinical decision: exhaustion and altered mental state, not a gas.
  • Ketamine, a cuffed tube, fluid first, pressor ready. Expect hypotension when positive pressure starts.
  • Low rate, small VT, I:E 1:4, expiratory time 4–5 s, plateau < 30, pH > 7.2.
  • Peak − plateau is resistance; plateau − PEEP is stretch. Watch the plateau.
  • External PEEP at or just below the intrinsic PEEP once he triggers; never above it.
  • Sudden collapse: disconnect first, then DOPE. Ultrasound before the X-ray.
  • Prevent auto-PEEP: long expiration, bronchodilators, sedation, low VT. Blockade shortest and lowest.
